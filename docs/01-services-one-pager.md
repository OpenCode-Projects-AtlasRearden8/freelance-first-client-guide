# Chapter 1: The services one-pager

Before a website, before a logo, before a business card you'll hand out exactly twice: write a services one-pager. It's a single page that tells a stranger what you do, who it's for, and what to do next.

## Why one page

Because nobody reads more. Not because people are lazy, but because they're deciding. A one-pager is a decision document. The easier you make their decision, the more often it lands in your favor.

## The seven parts

1. **Headline: what you do, in one line.** Bad: "Creative solutions for dynamic enterprises." Good: "I set up and maintain small-business bookkeeping in QuickBooks so you can see your numbers every Friday."
2. **Who it's for.** Name the person, not the demographic. "New freelance designers who just got their first invoice," not "entrepreneurs."
3. **The problem you remove.** Say it the way the client would whisper it to a friend: "I'm drowning in scope creep and my projects always run long."
4. **What you do (3–5 bullets).** Each bullet is a deliverable with a verb. "Build your site. Write your copy. Hand you a training video."
5. **Proof.** For a first-timer, proof is thin — that's OK. Use a background credential ("10 years in healthcare ops"), a small portfolio piece, or an honest line like "Limited availability while I build my client list — that means you get careful attention."
6. **What it costs — or how pricing works.** When you're new, "project pricing, typically $X–$Y for this type of work" beats nothing. Even a starting range signals seriousness. (Chapter 2 covers how to land on numbers you don't regret.)
7. **One clear next step.** "Reply to this email" or "Book a 20-minute intro call." One. Never two links and a QR code.

## Format tips

- Plain text or a simple PDF is fine. The words matter more than the font.
- Keep it genuinely to one page. If it's two, you haven't decided what the offer is yet.
- Write a different one-pager per audience if your skills span wildly different needs. "That same person" should be recognizable in all seven parts.

## Practice drill

Write a one-pager for an imaginary client of *this year's you*, then rewrite it pretending you're busy and slightly tired. The tired version is usually better — shorter sentences, fewer adjectives, wider spacing.

## What to do with it

- Pin it in your email drafts for replies to "so what do you do?"
- Send it as an actual attachment when someone asks for more info.
- Later, it becomes your website's home page with almost no changes. That's the hidden job of the one-pager: it's your future website, wearing its street clothes.
