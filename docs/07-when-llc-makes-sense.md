# Chapter 7: When an LLC makes sense

The LLC question arrives early, framed as either thrill ("do I get to make a company page for my Instagram?") or fear ("will I get sued?"). Real answer: an LLC is a **tool**, and you form one when the tool starts doing work for you.

:::disclaimer
This chapter is education only — never legal or tax advice. What follows is a plain-English map of the usual thinking; your situation deserves a professional in your state, especially when it comes to taxes.
:::

## What an LLC actually does

An LLC — a **limited liability company** — is a legal box that sits between you and the business. Roughly, the tools it hands you:

1. **A liability dinghy.** If the business is sued or can't pay a debt, the box takes the hit instead of your personal savings — *if* you operate it properly (a real bank account, contracts in the LLC's name, no fund-blending).
2. **A name to sign as.** Clients see "Dana Rivera, LLC" on invoices and agreements, which feels established because it is.
3. **A box for taxes.** By default a single-member LLC's taxes pass through to your personal return in the US; an accountant can tell you later whether a different tax election would help you. That election is a tax decision — ask a professional, not an internet thread.
4. **A cleaner aisle between personal and business money.** Even beginners notice the calm of a separate bank account.

## Signals it's time (in rough order)

- **You've signed, or are about to sign, your first real contract.** Paper with a company name on it belongs in a legal box's name.
- **The work kind of bleeds into regulated or risky territory** — anything touching people's money, health decisions (even education-adjacent!), or third-party systems.
- **You're hiring help** — subcontractors, an editor, a VA.
- **You want a name on a bank account** before income starts flowing, so account setup is calm, not rushed.
- **A state registration requirement** shows up where you live — some states want in-state freelancers registered with the relevant entity. Check with your state's filings page or a local advisor.

## Signals it's safe to wait

- Tiny jobs, friends and family as clients, nothing in writing yet, no personal data handled.
- You're still deciding whether freelancing is a season or a direction.

There's no shame in waiting; forming an LLC costs filing fees and an annual to-do list, and a too-early box is a hamster wheel of paperwork.

## First 90 days checklist once you do form one (general, not advice)

1. File the formation document with your state (that's choosing a name, a registered agent, and a filing fee).
2. Get an EIN from the IRS, free, online — do not pay anyone for this.
3. Open a business bank account; move nothing yet, just open it.
4. Start routing every business invoice through the entity, and every entity payment through the account.
5. Keep a simple bookkeeping file from day one — income, expenses, fee categories. (The companion **llc-first-year-handbook** covers the whole first year: registered agent, operating agreement, tax calendar, and the paperwork you'll actually touch.)
6. Re-read your client agreements to sign as the entity going forward.

## One honest footnote

The most common LLC mistake isn't paperwork sloppiness — it's trading under the entity while keeping money mixed with personal accounts, which quietly dissolves the protection. The bureaucracy of separation is boring; it's also the entire product. Keep the walls up.

## Short list of what an LLC is *not*

- Not insurance. Professional liability insurance is a separate tool and worth asking an agent about once real clients appear.
- Not a tax shelter. Nothing about an LLC lowers your taxes by itself — ask a professional about your actual situation.
- Not required to start freelancing. Many freelancers begin as sole proprietors and form an LLC at their first serious contract.
