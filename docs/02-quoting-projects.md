# Chapter 2: Quote by the project, not the hour

Fear of hourly pricing usually looks like this: you're scared of naming a number, so you hide behind "$X/hour" and hope the hours don't add up to something embarrassing. Hourly pricing isn't a pricing model — it's a coping mechanism with an invoice attached.

## Why hourly hurts you

- **It caps your income at your speed.** Get faster, earn less. That's backwards.
- **It invites surveillance.** Clients start watching your timer instead of your work.
- **It punishes the estimate error.** With project pricing, you bundle small overruns inside a fair estimate. With hourly, every overrun is an awkward conversation.
- **It asks the wrong question.** The client isn't buying your hours; they're buying *the problem being gone*.

## What to charge: the calm math

You do need to know your floor. Do this once, in a spreadsheet, and stop agonizing:

1. **Target monthly income** — what you need, not what you dream of.
2. **Billable hours per month** — of a 160-hour month, maybe 60–80 are truly billable once you count admin, marketing, and learning.
3. **Floor rate = target income ÷ billable hours**, rounded up somewhere that feels plausible. That's your *floor* — a minimum used to sanity-check, not a public number.

Project quotes then come from your own estimate of effort, checked against the floor: if the project price works out to less than floor-rate × estimated hours, the project is off-spec and needs to be bigger, simpler, or declined.

## The project quote, step by step (use the [quote worksheet](quote-worksheet.md))

1. **Define done.** Write one sentence for what the client will have when done: "A five-page WordPress site with their copy, hosted, and a contact form that emails them."
2. **List the parts.** Draft, review rounds, revisions, launch, training. Keep each part to a verb and a deliverable.
3. **Estimate each part honestly**, then add 30–50% buffer. New freelancers always estimate warm and optimistic; the buffer isn't padding, it's paying for the parts of the job you don't know exist yet.
4. **Price = effort + value.** Effort: floor rate × buffered hours. Value: what problem this removes for them — a site that wins them customers is worth more than hours suggest, and it's fair for your price to reflect that.
5. **Write the range, not the point.** "$1,400–$1,900 depending on copy source" gives the client agency and protects you.
6. **Payment terms in the quote.** The classic beginner-friendly pattern: **50% up front, 50% on delivery.** Yes, even for nice people. Especially for nice people.

## Example quote email

> Hi Priya — great to hear from you. For the five-page site with your copy, hosting setup, contact form, and a 30-minute handoff call, my quote is **$1,400–$1,900**, depending on how much copy editing the draft pages need. Timeline is about three weeks from start. Standard terms are 50% to book, 50% on delivery — invoice goes out same day each time.
>
> If that works, I'll send a short one-page agreement and the deposit invoice this week.

Notice what's *in* it: deliverables ("done"), a range, a timeline, terms, and a next step. What's *not* in it: hourly math, apologies, or a discount you invented out of nervousness.

## Raising your price later

Rewrite the one-pager's price line first, quote new work at the new rate, and grandfather existing clients gently. You'll be surprised how rarely anyone notices the first bump — clients compare you to the alternative (doing it themselves badly or not at all), not to your last quote.

## Common pricing failures and their fixes

| Failure | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Naming the price out loud before knowing the scope | "Let me look at the details and send a written quote by Friday." |
| Discounting when nervous | Add scope, not discounts — "I can trim it to three pages if you'd like a lower price." |
| Change orders billed at "whatever seems right" | Chapter 3's agreement includes a change-request line; use it. |
| Rush work at normal price | Rush fee: a clean 25–50% bump, quoted in writing up front. |
