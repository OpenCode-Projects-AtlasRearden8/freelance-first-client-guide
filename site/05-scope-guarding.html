Chapter 5: Kind scope-guarding

Scope creep is the slow widening of a project's edges — a logo becomes a favicon becomes a newsletter header becomes "while you're in there." It's rarely malicious. Clients are busy, and each request feels tiny to them. The cumulative effect can eat your weekend and your margin whole.

The guard is a sentence you already prepared

Don't improvise boundaries in the moment. Keep three sentences in your head like a first-aid kit:

  1. The "yes, and" fence. "Happy to do that — I'll treat it as a change request and send a small quote so we both know where we stand."
  2. The "later parking lot." "Good idea — let's note it for a phase two so the current scope keeps its ship date."
  3. The "inside vs outside" refresher. "That one's in scope; that other one I'd need to quote separately. I'm keeping this one at three weeks, not six."

All three protect scope while sounding generous. That's the trick: kindness in tone, firmness in structure. Never apologize for boundaries you documented — you just read the document out loud, with a smile.

Watches against the classic creeps

  • "While you're in there." The most expensive four words in freelancing. Any request beginning that way gets a quote, not a favor.
  • The endless revision round. Chapter 3's "up to 2 rounds" line exists for this. Round 3 becomes a new round of a separate revision agreement, quoted.
  • The meeting that refuses to end. "I need to hop to another call — I'll write follow-ups on today's points after lunch." Then send the follow-up doc; it is the deliverable that meeting was supposed to produce.
  • The bonus ask at delivery. A warm "while we're at it…" at the finish line gets the warm rope: same change-request line, quoted same day.

Why kind, not hard

  • You'll likely see this client again — next project, next referral next year.
  • The client's boss is often the invisible third person asking for the extra thing. Give your contact something they can forward to their boss ("here's the quote for the extra piece") instead of something they must defend.
  • Firmness practiced on small projects is cheap. Firmness learned on a five-figure project, expensive.

The parking lot doc

Every project should have a shared "later" list. When a request shows up, quote it or park it — one of the two. The parking lot is the politest invention in freelancing because the client's ideas are never dropped, they're just queued. And parked ideas become your next paid engagement's seed list.
