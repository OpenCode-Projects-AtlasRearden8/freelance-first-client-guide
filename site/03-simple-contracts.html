Chapter 3: Simple contracts without the lawyer-fear framing

The word "contract" makes new freelancers imagine parchment, Latin incantations, and a lawyer somewhere counting billable hours. Reality: a contract is simply a shared, written memory of what was agreed. That's it. That's the whole magic.

Heads-up: This chapter is education, not legal advice. It explains what agreements commonly cover so you can talk sense with clients and, when stakes get real, with a professional in your state. Forming or reviewing a real contract for your specific situation is a job for a licensed advisor.

Why a written agreement protects both of you

Clients are not protected from you by an agreement — they're protected along with you. Clear scope protects the client from surprise invoices. Clear payment terms protect you from surprise silence. Everything in the page below serves both sides.

The one-page agreement, section by section

Here's the plain-language skeleton I recommend, kept to about one page:

  1. Who and what. Names of the parties, and one sentence describing the work.
  2. Deliverables. The list from your quote — each item checks off when done.
  3. Timeline. Start date + target delivery, plus the honest caveat: "Timelines assume timely feedback within 3 business days."
  4. Payment. Amount, schedule (e.g. 50/50), method, and what happens if late — e.g. "work pauses after 10 days unpaid."
  5. Revisions and changes. A standard, generous-in-public, firm-in-document pattern: "Includes up to 2 rounds of revisions on each deliverable. Requests beyond that scope are quoted separately before work proceeds."
  6. Cancellation. What happens if either side stops: "You may cancel at any time with written notice. Work completed to that date is billed proportionally; the deposit is credited toward that total."
  7. Ownership. "On final payment, deliverables belong to the client. I keep the right to show the portfolio work, unless we agree otherwise." (Clients nearly always grant portfolio rights when it's expected rather than begged for.)
  8. "Not advice" / professional-lines note, if your field blurs into regulated territory (like health topics): a simple "Services are educational and operational, not medical, legal, or tax advice."

Ways people soften it

  • Email counts. A clear "Here's what I'll do, by when, for how much; reply 'Agreed' and I'll start" is a legitimate mini-agreement for tiny jobs.
  • Track-changes kindness. Invite the client to edit the agreement. A client who edits clause 6 to make cancellation fairer to you is a green-flag client.
  • Keep it readable. No ALL-CAPS thickets. Judge of a good agreement: both of you can find "what happens if it's late" in ten seconds.

Three phrases to keep in drafts

  • "Happy to make that a change request — I'll quote it separately so the timeline stays honest."
  • "Let's confirm the revision round in writing so I can start on it."
  • "Adding scope now would push delivery by about a week or take the piece down to two pages — your call."

When real stakes appear

If any of these show up, the friendly one-pager has done its job and it's time to consult a professional: a contract with indemnification clauses, a company asking you to sign their ten pages, work touching regulated data (health/finance), equity or revenue-share, or a job big enough that losing it would genuinely hurt your finances.
