Chapter 4: Invoices that get paid

An invoice is a document, not a request. Send it like paperwork, not like a plea.

The anatomy of an invoice that gets paid

  1. Your name and contact. (If you have an LLC, the invoice is from "Your Name, LLC" — see Chapter 7.)
  2. Invoice number. A never-changing scheme: YYYY-NNN. First invoice of 2026: 2026-001. Never reuse, ever.
  3. Date + due date, in words. "Payment due within 14 days of the invoice date." Never send an invoice without a due date — "whenever you get a chance" is not a date.
  4. Who to pay. Payment link or bank details exactly as they should enter them, with the business name matching your bank account.
  5. What it's for, in client-vocabulary. Match their words: "Website redesign — final milestone" not "Phase 2 deliverable completion."
  6. The amount, once, obvious. One total on top or in bold. If taxes apply to your situation, that's a question for a tax professional — the invoice just needs to show what they asked and what it totals.
  7. A kind line on purpose. "Thanks for the great project — the testimonial form is attached if you're up for it." (Chapter 6.)

The rhythm that works

  • Invoice on the milestone, same day. The day you deliver, the invoice goes out. Momentum is real; half-paid projects die of momentum loss.
  • One follow-up at the day after due. Friendly, unembarrassed: "Just noting the invoice is now past due — let me know if it needs to route to someone on your side." A surprisingly common cause of non-payment: the invoice is simply in the wrong inbox and nobody was told.
  • Second follow-up a week later, same tone, one sentence longer. Then pause work — calmly, in writing — if Chapter 3's agreement said you would.
  • Never use shame. Paid clients have been embarrassed by everything except a clear invoice and a calm reminder. Keep it boring.

The invoice checklist

Print or duplicate the invoice checklist and literally tick every line before you hit send. Nine out of ten "unpaid invoice" months are caused by an invoice going out with the due date missing, the amount ambiguous, or the email never landing in the right inbox.

If work is big and slow

For projects over a few weeks, break the invoice into milestone invoices matching delivery points rather than "pay me at the end." Milestone invoicing keeps cash moving, and it lets small terminations end cleanly without a big argument.
